01 / The idea

Can a few river samples tell us
what happened upstream?

We’re building a river model that works in both directions: predict the chemistry, then use measurements to estimate the processes that produced it.

This page explains the first experiment in the series (7 September 2026).
Since then we have fitted a small neural network instead of five fixed rates, and estimated chemical inputs, a storage anomaly and decay together. See the current briefing for those results.

A chemical enters from the land, mixes with upstream water, and some is removed or transformed along the river. Here “decay” means loss from the modeled tracer pool.

Inverse: “What rates explain the samples?”

Keep the inputs fixed. Adjust the decay rates until the model agrees with measured concentrations.

That second direction is the capability being tested here. In this experiment we fit five numbers; the neural-network version came later and is covered in the main briefing.

One equation, if you want it

chemical flux out = exp(−rate × residence time) × (local chemical input + upstream chemical flux)

Concentration = chemical flux ÷ water flow, with matching units. The toy gives every reach 10 m³/s of local water, 0.01 kg/s of local tracer, and one day of residence time. The real experiment uses the supplied routing times and the draft’s residence-time conversion. Changing the rate makes the mapping nonlinear.

02 / A practice exam with an answer key

First, test it in a world
where we know the answer.

An OSSE is a synthetic-observation experiment: invent the true rates, simulate measurements, then see whether the model can recover those rates.

1

Build a synthetic “truth”

Use a real 64,579-reach network. Split it into five west-to-east longitude classes, each with its own known decay rate. These classes are not catchment boundaries.

2

Make imperfect samples

Generate concentrations at 40 virtual gauges and add 20% relative noise. This is scatter, not a strict ±20% error bound.

3

Hide the answer from the fit

Start all five rates at 0.10/day. Let the optimizer see only 30 noisy training measurements, plus a prior expressing plausible rates.

4

Check the ten unseen locations

Did it recover the rates? Does it predict concentrations at gauges that were never used to choose the fit?

03 / A model that tells us how to improve its guess

JAX supplies the sensitivity.
The optimizer adjusts the rates.

For each rate, JAX calculates: “If I change this number a little, how does the mismatch with the observations change?” That sensitivity is called a gradient.

The fitting loop we have now

1. GuessFive decay rates
2. PredictRun the river model
3. ComparePredictions vs samples
↑ JAX gradient + optimizer → update the five rates

The objective combines disagreement with the samples and a penalty for implausible rates. That penalty is our prior; it helps keep a sparse-data fit sensible.

JAX differentiates the coded equations automatically. It does not decide which physics is correct. A conventional gradient-based optimizer makes the updates.

Physics still routes the river.
The neural network would learn a rate relationship—not replace the whole river model.

The experiment on this page fits five positive rates with JAX gradients and SciPy L-BFGS-B. It is not HMC and not a time-evolving 4D-Var experiment. The later neural-network and joint-estimation runs use the same gradients and optimiser.

05 / A useful first rung, not the whole ladder

We’re testing whether sparse data
can teach us river chemistry.

What this result supports

A differentiable river model can recover five spatially varying decay rates from a small set of noisy samples in this controlled setup.

It’s an encouraging check that the model, gradients, optimizer, and observation comparison work together.

What it does not establish

It does not show that we can infer all unknown inputs and processes from real observations.

The same model generated and fitted the data. Sources were known. We tested one seed. Model error, uncertain sources, storage dynamics, and neural networks remain outside this test.

The main catch: different causes can look alike

More chemical input
+ faster decay≈Less chemical input
+ slower decay→Similar downstream
concentration

That’s why we held inputs fixed. A neural network cannot create missing information; later we will need useful observations and constraints on the sources.

What happened next.

We replaced the five rates with a small neural network, and found that observation coverage — not the network — was the limiting factor: tripling the training gauges cut the median decay-rate error from 47% to 19%. We also estimated chemical inputs, a storage anomaly and decay together, where the storage term turned out to be driven mostly by the prior. The full briefing has both results and their limits.
